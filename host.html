How hosting works

Three steps, and most of the work is ours.

  1. We check the home and the town

    The electrical service, a spot in the yard, fiber at the street, the insurer, and what the town’s code allows, all before anyone signs anything.

  2. We install it

    Licensed electricians, the permits, the utility, the enclosure and the computer itself, all at our cost, with nothing for the home to buy.

  3. You host it, and we pay you rent

    A fixed monthly rent, heat in the winter and backup power, and nothing for you to run or look after, since we monitor and repair it remotely with a few visits a year.

Selling your home? The agreement ends and we remove the computer within days.

For the home

It starts to feel like a utility of its own.

We wanted the computer to earn its keep for the house as well as for us, and between the monthly rent, the heat and the battery, the home ends up with a great deal more than a machine in the yard.

Fixed rent, all year

We pay the same rent every month for the use of the space, whether the computer is busy or sitting idle (the rental risk is ours to carry).

Warmth in winter

The computer gives off up to 15 kW of heat around the clock, roughly what a small furnace puts out, and piped into the house it can carry much of the winter heating.

Power when the grid is down

A battery backs up the house, and when the grid goes down the computer shuts itself off first so that the house gets the power.

A good partner for solar

For homes with solar, the computer runs on the panels while the sun is up, and it may qualify for clean-energy incentives that a household can’t claim on its own.

Permission

We check with the town first.

Most town codes were written long before anyone imagined a computer like this at a house, so before any AI computer goes in we read the town’s code and, where it is silent, ask for a ruling, much the way the early solar companies did.

  1. Read the code

    Home-business rules, accessory equipment, and any data-center definition or ban.

  2. Ask for a ruling

    That the computer is accessory equipment, like a generator or an AC condenser.

  3. Meet the standards

    Setbacks, screening, permits, and the night noise limit at the property line.

Questions

Fair questions from a homeowner.

How big is it?

Bigger than an air conditioner. The server sits in a full-size equipment rack with its battery and power gear, inside a sound-treated enclosure with its own cooling, and we will publish the enclosure’s size once the first one is built.

Will the neighbors hear it?

We are designing it to stay under New Jersey’s nighttime limit at the property line (50 decibels, which is about the sound of a quiet office), and noise is the single biggest driver of the enclosure design.

What if the power goes out?

The battery backs up the house, and the computer shuts itself down first so that everything in the battery goes to the house.

Is it safe?

It is listed equipment on its own circuits, watched around the clock for heat and smoke and able to be shut off remotely, and we insure it, with a liability policy that covers the home.

Can it see our network?

No. It has its own fiber line and never joins the home network, and we never look at what our customers run on it.

Who pays for the internet?

We do. The computer has its own business fiber line in our name, separate from the household’s internet.

Do we need a bigger electrical service?

Usually not, if the home has a standard 200 A service. Smart power throttling watches the home’s total draw and turns the computer down first, so the house always gets what it needs and the service stays within its rating (the battery covers short peaks). A 100 A service leaves too little room.

Does it raise our electric bill?

No. The computer’s power is either on its own meter or included in the rent, and it never shows up on the household’s bill.

Do we have to do anything?

Not really. We monitor and repair it remotely with a few scheduled visits a year, and all we ask is that you keep the circuit on and let us in when we give notice.

What will it compute?

AI work for research teams and companies (training and running AI models, simulations and other heavy computing), and whatever it runs, it never touches your home or your network.

What if we sell the house?

The agreement ends, and we remove the computer within days.

Is our town allowed?

That is the first thing we check. If your town’s code is silent we ask for a ruling, and if your town has banned data centers we simply won’t place an AI computer there.

Thinking about hosting?
