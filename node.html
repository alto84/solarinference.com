The AI computer

What it takes to run one at home.

An AI computer is one full data center server, and running it in a home means building in everything a data center would give it, which is why we are starting with our own house and learning every part of it ourselves.

Inside

One server, eight GPUs, and everything around it.

Each one is a single AI computer with eight GPUs, the kind of machine the large AI data centers install by the rack, and we own it, install it, insure it and run it remotely, while AI teams rent its computing by the hour through online marketplaces or under longer contracts.

It has its own meter and its own fiber line, so it never touches the home’s network or the household’s electric bill.

Power

It shares the home’s service, and it gives way first.

At full power an AI computer draws about 15 kW, which is a lot for a house, so we don’t leave it to chance. A listed energy-management system watches the home’s total draw and turns the computer down first, so the house always gets what it needs and the service stays within its rating (the battery covers short peaks).

  • A standard 200 A service has room for an AI computer with throttling.
  • A 100 A service leaves too little room, so we won’t place an AI computer there.
  • Each town’s inspector has to accept the system before the computer goes in.
  • The street’s transformer is the utility’s call, so we ask them first.

Working with the utility

An AI computer is a large, steady load that the utility can see and control, and we would much rather plan each one with the utility than surprise them.

Service
Smart throttling fits the computer within the home’s existing service, and we check room on the street’s transformer.
Metering
Its own meter, so the power it uses is billed to us and never to the household.
Peak hours
The computer turns down when the grid is strained, and the battery can help carry the house.
Planning
We tell the utility where each computer goes, so the neighborhood is ready before it arrives.

Living with it

Heat, noise and safety are the real design work.

Power, heat and noise all behave differently in a house than in a data center, and most of the engineering in an AI computer goes into making a big machine a good neighbor.

Heat

Up to about 51,000 BTU per hour, around the clock, which is roughly a small furnace, and piped into the house it can carry much of the winter heating.

Noise

Up close, a server like this is about as loud as heavy traffic, so the enclosure is sound-treated, and our target is under New Jersey’s nighttime limit at the property line (50 decibels, about a quiet office).

Safety

Listed equipment on its own circuits, watched around the clock for heat and smoke and able to be shut off remotely, and we insure it, with a liability policy that covers the home.

Backup

A battery backs up the house, and when the grid goes down the computer shuts itself off first so that the house gets the power.

Privacy and security

Locked down, for the home and for our customers.

Its own line

The computer has its own fiber line and never joins the home’s network, so the household and our customers never share a connection.

Locked and watched

The equipment sits in a locked enclosure, watched around the clock for heat, smoke and tampering, and we can shut it off remotely.

Encrypted while it computes

The computer supports confidential computing, which keeps a customer’s data encrypted even while the GPUs are working on it, and we never look at what customers run.

How we build

A few homes, and ours first.

A small start, on purpose.

We are proving this out on a small number of homes (the hosting, the upkeep and the day-to-day running of it), and we won’t think about anything larger until those are working well.

Ours comes first.

We are building the first AI computer at our own home, since we would much rather learn the hard lessons ourselves than ask a host family to learn them with us.
